My whole k3s cluster runs as three VMs on one Proxmox host: a single mini PC, one NVMe disk, nothing else. That's a documented, deliberate constraint, and real zero-downtime HA isn't achievable on this hardware. What I had never measured, only assumed, was whether splitting that one host into three virtualized nodes cost more than it was worth compared with running k3s on fewer VMs or bare-metal on the host. So I measured it. Short answer: the split costs a few hundred MB of RAM per VM, and both incidents I blamed on it were caused by the single shared disk.

## Allocation vs. What's Actually Used

| Resource | Host total | k3s-11 | k3s-12 | k3s-13 | Sum allocated | % of host |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| vCPU | 16 threads | 6 | 4 | 4 | 14 | 87.5% |
| RAM | 62GiB | 16GiB | 8GiB | 8GiB | 32GiB | ~52% |

CPU allocation is tight on paper: 14 of 16 threads committed to the three VMs alone, leaving two threads' worth of nominal headroom for the Proxmox host itself and every LXC guest sharing this box (Backup server, Docker apps, the AI stack, both DMZ containers). RAM has far more slack.

But allocated isn't the same as used. `kubectl top nodes` in steady state, post-incident-recovery:

| Node | CPU used | CPU % | Memory used | Memory % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| k3s-11 | 675m | 11% | 10.4GiB | 66% |
| k3s-12 | 384m | 9% | 3.9GiB | 48% |
| k3s-13 | 271m | 6% | 5.0GiB | 63% |

Total in-cluster compute demand is small: roughly 1.3 cores out of 14 allocated vCPUs, under 10% on average. CPU is not the binding constraint day to day. Memory is comfortably provisioned and, unlike CPU, not overcommitted even on paper.

## The Actual Cost of Splitting Into Three VMs

All three VMs run with hardware virtualization extensions enabled, which puts raw KVM compute overhead in the low single digits of percent versus bare metal. That's well established and not worth re-benchmarking. The measurable per-VM cost is fixed: the QEMU process plus the guest kernel and OS baseline, on the order of a few hundred MB of RAM per VM. Against a 62GiB host, that's noise.

## What Two Real Incidents Actually Revealed

Two separate incidents on this host showed up the same way: contention across the shared LVM thin pool. One was an earlier storage-repair incident; the other was a `KubeAPIServerHighLatency` alert triggered by `trivy-operator` scans and a Renovate job running at the same time. `iostat` during the second incident showed **more than ten separate thin-pool logical volumes, one per guest virtual disk, all pegged near 100% utilization at once.**

That was the finding that mattered: splitting into three VMs gave me **zero disk I/O isolation** between them, because all three virtual disks still funnel through the same physical NVMe and the same thin pool's shared metadata and allocation path. What hurt me in these incidents was never "too many VMs". It was one physical disk serving everything, however many guests sit on top of it.

## Consolidating Wouldn't Have Fixed Either Incident

I considered collapsing to a single-node k3s, and to two VMs (merging the two workers). Both would reclaim the fixed per-VM overhead (maybe 500MB–1GB of RAM total and a fraction of a CPU core) and simplify day-to-day operations to one or two nodes instead of three.

Neither addresses the actual failure mode. A single-node k3s still keeps every piece of state (the embedded datastore, every PV) on the same physical disk, so consolidating wouldn't touch the shared-disk contention. It would *also* throw away the one real benefit the split gives me: with three VMs, k3s's node-level fault tolerance works. A kernel panic or OOM on one worker doesn't take the control plane down with it, which matches Kubernetes' own failure assumptions even though the hypervisor underneath remains a single point of failure. Each VM's OS can also be patched, drained, and rebooted independently. Merging to two VMs is the same trade, just smaller. My two workers are already right-sized to their load (9% and 6% CPU, 48% and 63% memory), so merging them wouldn't free meaningful capacity; it would only concentrate two modest workloads onto one VM.

Giving up real fault isolation to save single-digit percent of host capacity, while the contention problem was never caused by the VM count, is a bad trade.

## Decision: Keep the Three VMs, Watch CPU Allocation Instead

I'm keeping the topology as-is. The measured overhead of the split (a few hundred MB of fixed RAM per VM, negligible CPU tax under KVM hardware acceleration) is real but small, and it buys node-level fault isolation this hardware would otherwise never have. Both incidents I've had came from the single shared physical disk, and that problem already has its own, more targeted mitigations at the I/O-scheduling layer.

The actionable finding is on the CPU side, not the VM count. With 87.5% of host threads committed, CPU is the tighter of the two resources, and right-sizing each VM's core allocation against the low observed utilization (11%/9%/6%) frees host-level headroom without touching the three-VM structure. That's the same kind of targeted change that [staggering VM boot order](/blog/staggered-vm-boot-load-average-147/) and [tuning etcd's CPU priority](/blog/cpu-scheduling-etcd-proxmox-units/) already paid off on this host.
