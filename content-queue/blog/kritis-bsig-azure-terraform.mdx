Most KRITIS write-ups are aimed at Betreiber (operators) and their compliance officers: long PDFs full of "geeignete, verhältnismäßige und wirksame technische und organisatorische Maßnahmen" without a single line of code. That doesn't help the engineer who has to build the infrastructure a KRITIS auditor will later sign off on.

This article maps the technical obligations the BSI-Gesetz puts on operators of critical facilities to Azure resources, managed with Terraform, in the same Hub & Spoke architecture already covered on this blog.

If you learned KRITIS as "§8a BSIG": that section no longer exists. The NIS2 implementation act (NIS2UmsuCG) rewrote the BSIG in December 2025. The duties that used to sit in §8a are now spread across §30 (risk management for all NIS2 entities), §31 (additional requirements for Betreiber kritischer Anlagen, including attack detection), §32 (incident reporting) and §39 (proof of compliance). The section numbers below refer to the current BSIG.

> **Disclaimer:** This is not legal advice. KRITIS status itself is a threshold question (Betreiber kritischer Anlagen, determined by the sector thresholds, still taken from the BSI-KritisV until the new ordinance under the KRITIS-Dachgesetz replaces it) that a lawyer or compliance officer has to answer, not an infrastructure article. What follows covers the technical network and detection controls the BSIG names, not the organizational program around them (registration, proof documentation, sector-specific B3S standards).

## What the BSIG Requires from KRITIS Operators

Strip away the legal language and the BSIG asks four concrete things of a KRITIS operator's IT systems:

**1. Risk management measures at the state of the art (§30, §31 Abs. 1)**
Every NIS2 entity must take "appropriate, proportionate and effective" technical and organizational measures, and they should follow the "Stand der Technik" (§30 Abs. 1–2). Operators of critical facilities must go further wherever the effort is not out of proportion to the consequences of an outage (§31 Abs. 1). "Stand der Technik" is a moving target by design: what counted as state of the art in 2022 does not in 2026.

**2. Systems to detect attacks ("Systeme zur Angriffserkennung", SzA, §31 Abs. 2)**
Mandatory for KRITIS operators since May 2023 (then under §8a Abs. 1a) and carried over into the new law. The systems must continuously and automatically collect and evaluate relevant parameters from live operations, identify threats, and support remediation. A firewall that produces logs nobody reads does not qualify.

**3. Proof of compliance ("Nachweispflicht", §39)**
KRITIS operators must prove to the BSI, through audits, inspections or certifications, that the measures are in place. Under the old §8a this was every two years; §39 now requires it every three years, with the first deadline set by the BSI. Undocumented, unreproducible infrastructure fails this by default because there is nothing to show an auditor.

**4. Incident reporting (§32)**
Significant security incidents go to the joint reporting office of BSI and BBK in three stages: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, an incident notification within 72 hours, and a final report one month after that. You cannot report what your infrastructure never logged.

## Control 1: Segmentation as the Baseline

Nothing here differs from a well-built NIS2 deployment. Both start from the same premise: a flat network with unrestricted lateral movement cannot be "Stand der Technik" for critical infrastructure.

```hcl
resource "azurerm_network_security_group" "kritis_baseline" {
  name                = "nsg-kritis-baseline-${var.environment}"
  location            = var.location
  resource_group_name = var.resource_group_name

  security_rule {
    name                       = "Deny-Internet-Inbound"
    priority                   = 4096
    direction                  = "Inbound"
    access                     = "Deny"
    protocol                   = "*"
    source_address_prefix      = "Internet"
    destination_address_prefix = "*"
    source_port_range          = "*"
    destination_port_range     = "*"
  }
}

resource "azurerm_subnet_network_security_group_association" "spoke_bind" {
  subnet_id                 = azurerm_subnet.spoke_default.id
  network_security_group_id = azurerm_network_security_group.kritis_baseline.id
}
```

The difference from a generic hardening pass is that for KRITIS, this NSG configuration becomes evidence in the §39 audit. An auditor doesn't want a screenshot of the Azure Portal. They want to see that the control existed at deployment time and has stayed in place, and a `terraform plan` showing zero drift demonstrates exactly that.

## Control 2: Systeme zur Angriffserkennung (SzA)

Most infrastructure-as-code write-ups skip this control entirely, and it's the one that is mandatory for KRITIS operators. SzA requires continuous collection and analysis of security-relevant events. In Azure terms, that means flow logs shipped to a Log Analytics workspace, not sitting unused in a Storage Account.

Use VNet flow logs, not NSG flow logs. Azure has blocked the creation of new NSG flow logs since June 30, 2025, and retires them entirely on September 30, 2027. VNet flow logs attach to the virtual network and capture traffic for every subnet and NIC in it:

```hcl
resource "azurerm_network_watcher_flow_log" "spoke_flow_log" {
  network_watcher_name = azurerm_network_watcher.main.name
  resource_group_name  = azurerm_resource_group.rg.name
  name                  = "fl-spoke-${var.environment}"

  target_resource_id        = azurerm_virtual_network.spoke.id
  storage_account_id        = azurerm_storage_account.flow_logs.id
  enabled                   = true

  retention_policy {
    enabled = true
    days    = 90
  }

  traffic_analytics {
    enabled               = true
    workspace_id          = azurerm_log_analytics_workspace.soc.workspace_id
    workspace_region      = var.location
    workspace_resource_id = azurerm_log_analytics_workspace.soc.id
    interval_in_minutes   = 10
  }
}
```

`traffic_analytics` turns raw flow logs into something you can detect with. It enriches flows with Microsoft's threat intelligence, flags traffic to and from known-malicious IPs, and surfaces it in the Log Analytics workspace. That is what "Angriffserkennung" means in practice rather than in policy language. Azure offers processing intervals of 10 or 60 minutes. A system that has to justify its detection latency to an auditor should use 10.

Flow logs plus Traffic Analytics cover the network layer. A complete SzA in the BSI's sense usually also needs a SIEM layer (Microsoft Sentinel on the same workspace, for example) with detection rules and someone who responds to them.

Pair this with Azure Firewall's threat intelligence mode set to `Deny` rather than `Alert`. For KRITIS infrastructure, alerting on a known-malicious IP without blocking it falls short of the "Stand der Technik" bar:

```hcl
resource "azurerm_firewall" "kritis_fw" {
  name                = "fw-kritis-${var.environment}"
  sku_name            = "AZFW_VNet"
  sku_tier            = "Standard"

  threat_intel_mode = "Deny"
}
```

## Control 3: Nachweispflicht and the Evidence Trail

The §39 proof requirement is where infrastructure-as-code stops being a nice-to-have and becomes the compliance mechanism. Azure Policy assignments give you a running compliance dashboard instead of a point-in-time audit scramble:

```hcl
resource "azurerm_subscription_policy_assignment" "require_vnet_flow_logs" {
  name                 = "require-flow-logs"
  policy_definition_id = "/providers/Microsoft.Authorization/policyDefinitions/4c3c6c5f-0d47-4402-99b8-aa543dd8bcee"
  subscription_id      = data.azurerm_subscription.current.id
  display_name         = "Audit flow logs configuration for every virtual network"
}
```

Every Terraform apply traces back to a timestamped, attributable Git commit. Every policy assignment keeps verifying that the control is still in place after deployment. When the three-year audit comes around, the evidence is `git log` plus the Azure Policy compliance report, not a fire drill reconstructing what was deployed two years ago.

## Control 4: The Incident Reporting Clock

The 24-hour early warning under §32 runs from the moment you become aware of a significant incident. You only become aware if something logged it, so Diagnostic Settings on every security-relevant resource, feeding one workspace, are a prerequisite:

```hcl
resource "azurerm_monitor_diagnostic_setting" "fw_diagnostics" {
  name                       = "diag-fw-${var.environment}"
  target_resource_id         = azurerm_firewall.kritis_fw.id
  log_analytics_workspace_id = azurerm_log_analytics_workspace.soc.id

  enabled_log {
    category = "AzureFirewallApplicationRule"
  }
  enabled_log {
    category = "AzureFirewallNetworkRule"
  }
  enabled_log {
    category = "AzureFirewallDnsProxy"
  }
}
```

Without this, the first question an incident report has to answer ("when did it start, and what happened?") has no reliable source of truth. Diagnostic Settings applied uniformly across every resource in the Hub & Spoke, not just the ones that seemed important at design time, make that question answerable after the fact.

## What This Covers and What It Doesn't

This addresses the technical network security and detection controls behind §§30–32 and §39 BSIG. Full KRITIS compliance also requires:

- **Determining KRITIS status itself:** the sector thresholds, a legal and organizational question this article does not touch
- **Registration:** critical facilities register under §8 KRITIS-Dachgesetz (§33 Abs. 2 BSIG), with contact details kept current
- **Sector-specific security standards (B3S):** an optional route under §30 Abs. 9 to define what "Stand der Technik" means for your sector, usually going well beyond generic network controls
- **The organizational incident response process** around the technical logging covered above
- **Physical resilience and personnel security**, which the KRITIS-Dachgesetz and §30 address outside the scope of cloud infrastructure
