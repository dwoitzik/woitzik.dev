Anyone who runs a homelab or a small production deployment has heard of the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of your data, on two different media types, with one copy offsite. Fewer people can explain why each number is there, and that's how you end up with a setup that meets the letter of the rule and misses its purpose.

I've already written up my implementation: [Velero backing up to Garage S3](/blog/velero-garage-k3s-backup/) inside the cluster, and the [restore test that found a network conflict](/blog/backup-restore-test-never-verified-network-conflict/) the documentation never mentioned. This article is the layer underneath those posts: what 3-2-1 protects against, and how homelabs break it without realizing.

## Why Three Copies

The "3" is about the probability of simultaneous failure. One copy has no redundancy: a bad `apt upgrade`, a failing disk or a fat-fingered `rm -rf` is unrecoverable. Two copies survive a single failure, but only if the two can fail independently, which is the detail most setups get wrong (more on that below). With three copies and independent failure modes, "everything fails at once" becomes rare enough to plan around.

The number itself matters less than the idea behind it: enough copies that no single event can take out all of them before someone notices, including a slow one like ransomware encrypting files gradually.

## Why Two Media Types

This is the least obvious clause and the one most homelabs skip. The original intent was literal media (disk vs. tape vs. optical), but the point is two failure domains without a common cause. A primary NVMe and a backup on a second NVMe in the same machine share a power supply, a filesystem driver, and often the same firmware bug from the same batch. For backup purposes they are closer to one copy than two.

Diversity in practice looks like this: local disk for fast day-to-day recovery, an external HDD or tape that doesn't share the failure modes of the primary array, and cloud object storage that survives anything physical happening to the building. Each layer is there so it doesn't share a failure mode with the others.

## Why One Copy Offsite

This clause covers the scenario everyone underestimates: losing the physical location. Fire, flood, theft, or the mundane homelab version, a botched electrical job that takes out every device on the circuit at once. A backup in the same room as the thing it protects satisfies "3-2" and misses the point of "1", because the event most likely to destroy your primary data (something physical happening where you live) destroys every local copy with it.

"Offsite" doesn't have to mean another continent. A cloud object store, a drive at a relative's house or a second location you control all count. The test: does the event that destroys copy one also reach copy three?

## The Homelab-Specific Way This Rule Gets Violated

The subtlest failure, and the first one I'd flag for anyone building a homelab backup, is **backing up to a target that depends on the thing you're protecting**. An S3-compatible bucket running as a pod inside the Kubernetes cluster it backs up looks like a second copy on a second medium until the cluster fails and takes the "backup" with it. Design around that circularity up front instead of discovering it during an outage. An in-cluster object store is a useful layer for fast namespace-level restores, but it can't be the whole strategy.

The second gap is **backups that complete without containing data**. A job that reports "success" for weeks while capturing no file contents (because the flag for filesystem-level backup wasn't set, or a credential rotated without the job being updated) is worse than no backup, because it creates confidence exactly where you shouldn't have any. The only real defense is a scheduled test restore, not a green checkmark on the job.

The third is **treating "offsite" as a checkbox instead of a tested path**. An offsite copy you've never restored from is a hypothesis. Rate limits, expired credentials or a network path that only existed during initial setup all surface when you try to pull data back under pressure, which is the worst time to find them.

## A Practical Checklist

- **Copy 1 (primary):** your live data, wherever it actually lives day to day.
- **Copy 2 (local, different media):** a directly attached drive on a different bus than the primary. I run a [Seagate 2TB Portable HDD\*](/go/4vxwLpY) over USB because it shares no controller, bus or disk technology with the internal NVMe it backs up. It still lives in the same building, which is what copy 3 is for.
- **Copy 3 (offsite):** cloud object storage or a drive physically elsewhere, with a documented and periodically tested restore path.
- **Verification:** a scheduled job that doesn't just check "backup completed" but checks "backup contains the expected data," plus a full test restore on a cadence you actually keep.
- **Independence check:** for each pair of copies, ask what single event would take out both. If the answer is "the cluster, host or building failing", that pair doesn't satisfy the rule, however it looks on paper.

## The Result

Each clause of 3-2-1 maps to a failure mode: simultaneous failure (three copies), shared failure domains (two media types) and total site loss (one offsite). Homelabs rarely get it wrong by skipping backups altogether. They get it wrong by building something that looks like 3-2-1 on a diagram while two of the three copies share a failure domain, and a real restore test is what exposes that.
