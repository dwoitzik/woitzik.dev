Everyone who runs a homelab or a small production deployment has heard of the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of your data, on two different media types, with one copy offsite. Fewer people can actually explain why each of those three numbers exists, which is exactly how you end up with a backup strategy that satisfies the letter of the rule while failing the reason it exists.

I've written up the specific implementation on this blog already - [Velero backing up to Garage S3](/blog/velero-garage-k3s-backup/) inside the cluster, and the [restore test that found a network conflict](/blog/backup-restore-test-never-verified-network-conflict/) the documentation never mentioned. This article is the conceptual layer underneath those posts: what 3-2-1 is actually protecting against, and the specific ways homelabs violate it without realizing.

## Why Three Copies

The "3" isn't superstition - it's math about the probability of simultaneous failure. One copy has no redundancy at all; a single bad `apt upgrade`, a failing disk, or a fat-fingered `rm -rf` is unrecoverable. Two copies survive a single failure, but only if the second copy's failure is truly independent of the first's - which is the detail most setups get wrong (more on that below). Three copies, with independent failure modes, is the point where "everything fails at once" becomes rare enough to actually plan around instead of dreading.

The number that matters is not literally 3 - it's "enough copies that a single event, including a slow-moving one like a ransomware infection encrypting files gradually, cannot take out all of them before someone notices."

## Why Two Media Types

This is the rule's least obvious clause, and the one most homelabs skip entirely. "Two media types" doesn't mean two hard drives - it means two failure domains that don't share a common cause. A primary NVMe drive and a backup on a second NVMe drive in the same enclosure share a power supply, a filesystem driver, and often a batch of the same firmware bug. They are, for backup purposes, closer to one copy than two.

Real media-type diversity looks like: local disk (fast, for day-to-day recovery) plus an external HDD or tape (slow, but immune to the failure modes that take out the primary array) plus cloud object storage (immune to anything physical happening to the building). Each layer exists specifically to not share a failure mode with the others.

## Why One Copy Offsite

This is the clause that exists purely for the scenario everyone underestimates: total loss of the physical location. Fire, flood, theft, or - the mundane homelab version - a botched electrical project that takes out every device on the same circuit at once. A backup sitting in the same room as the thing it's protecting satisfies "3-2" but fails the entire point of "1," because the exact event most likely to destroy your primary data (something physical happening to where you live) destroys every local copy simultaneously.

"Offsite" doesn't have to mean a different continent. A cloud object store, a drive at a relative's house, or a second physical location you control all satisfy the requirement - the test is simply: does the failure mode that destroys copy one also reach copy three?

## The Homelab-Specific Way This Rule Gets Violated

The subtlest version of this failure, and the one I'd flag first for anyone building a homelab backup strategy, is **backing up to a target that depends on the thing you're protecting**. An S3-compatible bucket running as a pod inside the same Kubernetes cluster it's backing up looks like a second copy and a second media type - until the cluster itself fails, at which point the "backup" fails with it. That specific circularity is worth designing around explicitly rather than discovering during an actual outage; it's the reason an in-cluster object store is a useful layer for fast namespace-level restores, but cannot be the whole strategy on its own.

A second common gap: **backups that complete without verifying they contain data**. A backup job reporting "success" for weeks while silently capturing zero actual file contents (because a flag governing filesystem-level backup wasn't set, or a credential rotated without updating the job) is worse than no backup at all - it creates false confidence exactly where you need the opposite. The only real defense is a scheduled test restore, not just a green checkmark on the backup job itself.

A third: **treating "offsite" as a checkbox instead of a tested path**. An offsite copy that has never actually been restored from is a hypothesis, not a backup. Rate limits, expired credentials, or a network path that only exists during initial setup and quietly breaks later are all things that only surface when you try to pull data back down under pressure - which is the worst possible time to discover them.

## A Practical Checklist

- **Copy 1 (primary):** your live data, wherever it actually lives day to day.
- **Copy 2 (local, different media):** a directly attached drive on a different bus/power path than the primary - I run a [Seagate 2TB Portable HDD*](/go/4vxwLpY) connected via USB specifically because it has zero shared failure surface with the internal NVMe it's backing up.
- **Copy 3 (offsite):** cloud object storage or a drive physically elsewhere, with a documented and periodically tested restore path.
- **Verification:** a scheduled job that doesn't just check "backup completed" but checks "backup contains the expected data," plus a full test restore on a cadence you actually keep.
- **Independence check:** for each pair of copies, ask explicitly what single event would take out both. If the answer is "the cluster/host/building failing," that pair doesn't satisfy the rule regardless of how it looks on paper.

## The Result

3-2-1 isn't a box-ticking exercise - every clause maps to a specific failure mode it exists to survive: simultaneous failure (three), shared failure domains (two media types), and total site loss (one offsite). The homelab version of getting this wrong isn't usually skipping backups entirely - it's building something that looks like 3-2-1 on a diagram while quietly sharing a failure domain between two of the three copies, which is exactly the gap a real restore test is designed to expose.
