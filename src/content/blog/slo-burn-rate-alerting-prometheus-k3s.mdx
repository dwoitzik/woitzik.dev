Most uptime alerts look like this:

```yaml
- alert: ServiceDown
  expr: probe_success == 0
  for: 2m
```

That fires when a service is completely down for two minutes. It won't fire when a service answers 95% of requests for 48 hours straight, even though that burns through the entire monthly error budget in well under a day.

Burn-rate alerting works differently. Instead of alerting on current state, it alerts on **how fast you're spending your error budget**. Over a 30-day SLO window, a 30x burn rate exhausts the whole month's budget in one day. A 6x burn rate leaves about five days. Both need action, but different kinds.

This is the implementation running on my bare-metal k3s cluster ([BMAX Mini PC\*](/go/4bv3yF1) with 64GB), based directly on the multi-window multi-burn-rate approach from the [Google SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/). I also track server power draw via a [Tapo P110\*](/go/3STKPwD): useful for correlating outages with power events.

## Error Budgets, Briefly

If your SLO is 99.9% availability, your monthly error budget is the allowed downtime: 43.8 minutes in an average month (0.1% of roughly 43,800 minutes).

**Not all errors are equally urgent.** A 30x burn rate means a 3% error rate. That spends the 43.8-minute budget in about 24 hours, and a two-hour window at that rate has already used 8% of the month's budget. That's a page. A service burning at 6x (0.6% errors) needs five days to exhaust the budget. That's a ticket, handled during the shift.

Threshold alerting conflates these. Burn-rate alerting separates them.

## The SLI: HTTP Probe Success Rate

Everything is built on a single Service Level Indicator: the fraction of successful HTTP probes from the Prometheus [blackbox exporter](https://github.com/prometheus/blackbox_exporter).

The blackbox exporter probes each public service endpoint on a fixed interval. `probe_success` is 1 for a successful probe and 0 for a failure. The SLI is the average over a time window:

```yaml
# kubernetes/system/monitoring/slo-rules.yml

- record: job_instance:probe_success:rate5m
  expr: avg_over_time(probe_success[5m])

- record: job_instance:probe_error:rate5m
  expr: 1 - avg_over_time(probe_success[5m])
```

`1 - success_rate = error_rate`. At a 99.9% SLO, the allowed steady-state error rate is 0.001 (0.1%).

## Recording Rules: Pre-Computing the Windows

Multi-window alerting needs error rates computed over multiple time windows. Prometheus can do this inline in alert expressions, but pre-computing them as recording rules keeps the alert expressions readable and reduces query load.

```yaml
- name: slo.availability.windows
  interval: 1m
  rules:
    # Short windows (fast-burn detection)
    - record: job_instance:probe_success:rate1h
      expr: avg_over_time(probe_success[1h])
    - record: job_instance:probe_success:rate2h
      expr: avg_over_time(probe_success[2h])

    # Medium windows
    - record: job_instance:probe_success:rate6h
      expr: avg_over_time(probe_success[6h])
    - record: job_instance:probe_success:rate30m
      expr: avg_over_time(probe_success[30m])

    # Long windows (slow-burn detection)
    - record: job_instance:probe_success:rate24h
      expr: avg_over_time(probe_success[24h])
```

These evaluate every minute. Together with the 5m rule, that gives pre-computed availability across six windows, from 5 minutes to 24 hours.

## The Alert Rules

### Fast Burn: Page Immediately

```yaml
- alert: SLOAvailabilityFastBurn
  expr: |
    (1 - job_instance:probe_success:rate2h) > (30 * (1 - 0.999))
    and
    (1 - job_instance:probe_success:rate1h) > (30 * (1 - 0.999))
  for: 2m
  labels:
    severity: critical
    slo: availability
  annotations:
    summary: "SLO fast burn: {{ $labels.instance }}"
    description: >
      {{ $labels.instance }} error rate is burning through the monthly error budget
      at ≥30x the allowed rate. At this pace the 30-day 99.9% budget is exhausted in about 1 day.
      Current 2h error rate: {{ $value | humanizePercentage }}
```

**The math:** A 99.9% SLO means 0.1% of requests can fail. The threshold for 30x burn is `30 × 0.001 = 0.03`, a 3% error rate. If both the 2-hour and the 1-hour window exceed 3%, this fires. `$value` is the left-hand side of the `and`, the 2h error rate as a fraction (0.03, not 3), so the annotation formats it with `humanizePercentage` instead of appending a `%` sign to the raw number.

**Two windows, two jobs.** The long window (2h) makes sure a meaningful share of the budget is actually gone, so a two-minute blip can't page you. The short window (1h) confirms the burn is still happening, so the alert stops firing soon after the service recovers instead of staying red for the rest of the long window. Both must exceed the threshold at the same time. The SRE Workbook pairs a 1h long window with a 5m short one; my 2h/1h pair is slower to reset but less twitchy on a small set of probes.

**Burn-rate math at 30x:**
- A burn rate of 1 exhausts the budget exactly at the end of the 30-day window
- At 30x, time to exhaustion is 30 days ÷ 30 = 1 day
- Budget already spent when the 2h window trips: 30 × 2h ÷ 720h ≈ 8.3%

A day of runway and 8% already gone. Page.

### Slow Burn: Create a Ticket

```yaml
- alert: SLOAvailabilitySlowBurn
  expr: |
    (1 - job_instance:probe_success:rate6h) > (6 * (1 - 0.999))
    and
    (1 - job_instance:probe_success:rate30m) > (6 * (1 - 0.999))
  for: 15m
  labels:
    severity: warning
    slo: availability
  annotations:
    summary: "SLO slow burn: {{ $labels.instance }}"
    description: >
      {{ $labels.instance }} error rate is burning through the monthly error budget
      at ≥6x the allowed rate. At this pace the 30-day 99.9% budget is exhausted in about 5 days.
      Current 6h error rate: {{ $value | humanizePercentage }}
```

**The math:** `6 × 0.001 = 0.006`, a 0.6% error rate. At a sustained 6x burn rate, the monthly budget lasts 30 ÷ 6 = 5 days. That's enough runway that a ticket during the shift is the right response, not a page. `for: 15m` means the condition must hold for 15 minutes before firing, which filters transient dips.

6h (long) and 30m (short) windows. A slow degradation shows up over 6 hours; the 30m window keeps the alert from firing on an incident that has already ended.

Severity: `warning`. My Alertmanager only pushes `severity=critical` (plus a short list of named alerts) to Discord, so the slow burn shows up in Alertmanager and Grafana without a notification. Fix it during the shift.

## Comparing Against Threshold Alerting

| Scenario | `ServiceDown` (`probe_success == 0` for 2m) | Burn-rate alerts |
|---|---|---|
| Fully down for 10 minutes | Fires | Fast burn fires (8% of the 2h window failed) |
| Fully down for 2 minutes | Fires, barely | No page: 1.7% over 2h is below the 3% threshold |
| Intermittent failures, 95% success for 48h | Never fires (never down 2 minutes straight) | Fast burn fires (5% errors is a 50x burn) |
| 1% error rate for 6h | Never fires | Slow burn fires (above 0.6%) |
| Single 10-second blip | Doesn't fire (`for: 2m`) | Doesn't fire |

Burn-rate alerting catches partial degradations that an up/down alert never sees, and it stays quiet for short outages that barely dent the budget.

## Deploying as a PrometheusRule

The rules deploy as a `PrometheusRule` CRD, picked up automatically by the Prometheus Operator:

```yaml
apiVersion: monitoring.coreos.com/v1
kind: PrometheusRule
metadata:
  name: homelab-slo-alerts
  namespace: monitoring
  labels:
    prometheus: kube-prometheus
    role: alert-rules
    release: kube-prometheus-stack
spec:
  groups:
    - name: slo.burn-rate.page
      rules:
        - alert: SLOAvailabilityFastBurn
          # ... (see above)
```

The labels must match the `ruleSelector` on your `Prometheus` resource. With kube-prometheus-stack the default selector is `release: <release-name>`, which is `release: kube-prometheus-stack` here. My rules were deployed for a while with only the `prometheus` and `role` labels, and Prometheus never loaded them: no error, the alerts just never evaluated. `kubectl get prometheusrule -n monitoring` only proves the object exists. To see what Prometheus actually loaded, query its rules API through the service proxy (the port is part of the path): `kubectl get --raw /api/v1/namespaces/monitoring/services/kube-prometheus-stack-prometheus:9090/proxy/api/v1/rules`.

## What the Error Budget Dashboard Shows

The complementary Grafana dashboard (`slo-dashboard.yml`) renders three panels:

1. **Availability over time:** `job_instance:probe_success:rate5m` across all probed services
2. **Error budget remaining:** `100 * (1 - (1 - avg_over_time(probe_success[30d])) / (1 - 0.999))`, the share of the 0.1% budget left over 30 days. `probe_success` is a gauge, so this uses `avg_over_time`; `rate()` only makes sense on counters.
3. **Burn rate:** `(1 - job_instance:probe_success:rate1h) / (1 - 0.999)`, drawn against flat lines at 6x and 30x

The budget panel is the most useful. When it drops steeply, something is spending budget faster than the steady rate the SLO allows. That's a signal even before an alert fires.

## Limitations

This implementation measures **availability** only, using HTTP probes against the service endpoints from inside the cluster. It won't catch:

- Increased latency that doesn't fail probes (need histogram SLIs for that)
- Internal service-to-service degradation (need distributed tracing or internal probes)
- Correctness issues: a 200 OK with wrong data doesn't fail a probe

For most homelab services (Nextcloud, Authelia, Jellyfin, Gitea), availability is the right SLI. For a production API, you'd add latency SLOs (for example P99 under 500 ms) using histogram recording rules.

---

Swap Prometheus for Azure Load Balancer health probes or Application Gateway and the SLI stays the same: probe success rate. The recording rules and alert thresholds transfer as-is; only the metric source changes.
