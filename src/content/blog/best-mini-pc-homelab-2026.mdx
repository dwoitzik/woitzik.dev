## Selection Criteria: What Matters in a Homelab Mini PC

A homelab mini PC has different priorities than a desktop replacement:

**Idle power draw.** This machine runs 24/7. Every watt of idle draw is 8.76 kWh a year, so a 10W gap is roughly 88 kWh, or about €30 per year at €0.35/kWh. Intel N100 platforms consistently win here, idling at 6-10W versus 15-25W for Ryzen alternatives.

**Thermal management under sustained load.** Mini PCs live in racks or shelves with limited airflow. A chip that throttles after 20 minutes of sustained compilation is useless for building container images or running batch jobs.

**RAM expandability.** 16GB is the minimum for a serious Proxmox host running multiple VMs. Prefer models that accept SO-DIMM upgrades to 32GB or 64GB, not soldered memory.

**Network interfaces.** A single 1GbE port is a bottleneck for any hypervisor. Dual NICs, or at least one 2.5GbE port, make the machine viable as a Proxmox node without USB Ethernet dongles.

**Storage options.** M.2 NVMe is table stakes. A second drive bay (2.5" SATA or a second M.2 slot) for Proxmox local storage or TrueNAS is a major advantage.

**Noise.** If this thing sits in your office, fan noise at load matters. Some N100 boxes are fanless; most Ryzen units need active cooling under load.

### 1. [Beelink S12 Pro*](/go/4gGnuZ3): Best Overall

Intel N100 · 16GB DDR4 · 500GB SSD · €350-370

The S12 Pro hits the sweet spot for a homelab node. The Intel N100 is a 6W TDP chip, and the box idles around 7-9W at the wall, which barely registers on the electricity bill when running 24/7. The listing I'm linking ships with 16GB DDR4 and a 500GB M.2 SSD, which is enough for the Proxmox OS plus a reasonable local datastore on day one. There is also a 2.5" SATA bay for a second drive.

The LAN port needs a caveat. Beelink's own spec for the S12 Pro listed a single 1000Mbps (1GbE) port, and that product page is no longer on bee-link.com. DDR4 versus DDR5 makes little practical difference here: the N100 runs single-channel memory either way, and four efficiency cores are rarely bandwidth-bound in service workloads.

### 2. [Beelink SER5 MAX*](/go/4yvrdiy): Best Performance

For containerized workloads such as building Docker images, running Kubernetes operators, or hosting development databases, those extra cores mean faster builds and lower latency.

If this machine is your primary Proxmox host running 10+ containers, the performance justifies it. If it is just running Pi-hole and a couple of lightweight services, the N100 is the smarter buy.

Two things to know before you commit. First, the 16GB here is **LPDDR5 soldered on the board**. Unlike the DDR4 SO-DIMM in the S12 Pro, it cannot be swapped. The 500GB NVMe drive can be replaced with a larger one, but the RAM is fixed. Second, it has a single 2.5GbE port, so the same single-NIC caveat applies.

**Cons:**

**Buy the Beelink S12 Pro if:** You want an efficient all-rounder that handles Proxmox, containers, and lightweight VMs without fuss. 16GB and a 500GB drive in the box and the lowest power draw here. Check the NIC speed on arrival (see above). This is the default recommendation.

**Buy the Beelink SER5 MAX if:** You need more CPU power than the N100 and can live with fixed soldered memory. 8 cores / 16 threads is the right amount of headroom for a primary Proxmox host running a mix of containerized and VM workloads.

**Buy the GMKtec M5 Ultra if:** You need the dual 2.5GbE NICs and a memory upgrade path. **Buy the Intel NUC 13 Pro if:** Build quality and NIC reliability matter more than price. The Intel i226-V and two upgradeable SO-DIMM slots make this the "set and forget" choice for a production homelab that you do not want to babysit. The linked i5-1340P is non-vPro, so pick a vPro SKU separately if you need AMT out-of-band management.

**If you are starting from scratch:** Buy two Beelink S12 Pro units and run Proxmox with a VM or container failover strategy. Two N100 nodes cost less than a single Intel NUC 13 Pro and give you hardware redundancy that no single mini PC can provide. For automatic Proxmox HA you need a third vote: a third node or an external QDevice (a Raspberry Pi works).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much RAM do I need for a homelab mini PC?

16GB is the practical minimum if you are running Proxmox with more than one VM or container stack. For k3s with a few worker pods and monitoring (Prometheus, Grafana), 32GB gives you breathing room. 64GB only makes sense if you are running local LLM inference or multiple heavy databases. The N100 is officially limited to 16GB, the SER5 MAX's 16GB is fixed, and the M5 Ultra and NUC 13 Pro go to 64GB.

### Should I care about DDR4 vs DDR5 in a mini PC?

For N100 chips the difference is negligible in homelab service workloads, because the platform is single-channel and rarely bandwidth-bound. What matters far more on these units is whether the memory is **upgradeable**. The S12 Pro, M5 Ultra, and NUC 13 Pro all use DDR4 SO-DIMMs you can replace (the S12 Pro already ships at its 16GB ceiling); the SER5 MAX's LPDDR5 is soldered, so 16GB is fixed. Check that before you buy, not after.

### Can a mini PC replace a full-size server for homelab use?

For 80% of homelab use cases, yes. A single Ryzen 7 mini PC with 32GB RAM can run Proxmox with 5-8 VMs, a k3s cluster, monitoring, and local storage, which covers most self-hosting setups. What it cannot replace: a high-capacity NAS (you still need separate disks), GPU-accelerated workloads (no discrete GPU), and high availability (Proxmox HA needs three votes: three nodes, or two plus a QDevice).

### What about noise levels?

The N100-based S12 Pro is nearly silent at idle and quiet under moderate load. You will not hear it from a meter away. The SER5 MAX and M5 Ultra have audible fans under sustained load; the M5 Ultra is the loudest of the group. If noise is a primary concern, the S12 Pro or the Intel NUC 13 Pro are the quietest options with meaningful compute.

### Should I buy one powerful mini PC or multiple cheaper ones?

Multiple cheaper ones, almost always. One box is a single point of failure no matter how powerful it is. Two Beelink S12 Pro units (roughly €700-740 together) give you hardware redundancy and the ability to migrate VMs between hosts during maintenance. The exception is when you need 32GB and two NICs on a single box. In that case the GMKtec M5 Ultra with a 2x16GB kit is the minimum viable platform.

### Do these mini PCs support Proxmox well?

All of them work with Proxmox. The Intel NUC 13 Pro has the smoothest experience thanks to the Intel i226-V NIC and full kernel support. Budget boxes with Realtek 2.5GbE NICs work fine with Proxmox but may need the `r8169` driver tuned under heavy bridged traffic. The Ryzen platforms (Beelink, GMKtec) work well out of the box. I have personally run Proxmox on all of these platforms without blocking issues.

### What about the Raspberry Pi 5 as a homelab node?

The Raspberry Pi is a legitimate homelab option for lightweight, low-power roles. I run two <AffiliateLink href="/go/4wNS0oI">Raspberry Pi 4Bs</AffiliateLink> for [edge DNS and Keepalived failover](/blog/unbound-adguard-keepalived-homelab/) in my own lab, and if I were building that today I'd go straight for the <AffiliateLink href="/go/4wETNfv">Raspberry Pi 5</AffiliateLink> instead (that article explains why). Either way, it does not compete with mini PCs for compute-intensive workloads. The Pi 5's ARM Cortex-A76 cores are roughly a third slower than an N100 in single-threaded work, Proxmox VE has no official ARM build, and some Docker images still ship x86-only. For lightweight services, the Pi 5 wins on power efficiency (idle ~3-4W). For anything beyond DNS, reverse proxy, and monitoring, a mini PC is the better investment.
