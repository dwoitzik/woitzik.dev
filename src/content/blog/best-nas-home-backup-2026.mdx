## Selection Criteria: What Matters in a Home Backup NAS

A NAS for home backup has different priorities than one for media streaming or enterprise storage.

**Reliability over features.** You need a device that stays online 24/7, handles power loss gracefully, and does not corrupt your data during firmware updates. Synology leads here by a wide margin. QNAP's feature set is broader, but its update track record is rougher.

**Disk compatibility.** Not every enclosure works well with every drive. Synology's compatibility list is restrictive (the company pushes its own HAT5300 drives), though that conservatism means fewer surprise failures. With DSM 7.3 (October 2025), Synology walked back part of the 2024 lockout: third-party 3.5" HDDs and 2.5" SATA SSDs work again on 2025-generation Plus models without being blocked at install or pool creation. M.2 NVMe SSDs are still restricted to validated drives. QNAP and TerraMaster remain more permissive across the board.

**Backup software.** The native backup applications matter more than the hardware. Synology Active Backup for Business, Hyper Backup, and Snapshot Replication are the best in the consumer NAS space. QNAP's Hybrid Backup Sync is powerful but more complex. TerraMaster's and UGREEN's software is younger and less proven.

**RAID support.** For 2-bay units, RAID 1 (mirroring) is the minimum. For 4-bay units, RAID 5 or RAID 6 gives you redundancy plus capacity. Synology Hybrid RAID (SHR) lets you mix drive sizes without wasting space, which matters when you upgrade drives one at a time.

**Power consumption.** The NAS runs 24/7, so an idle draw of 10W versus 25W adds up over a year. Disk count matters more than the enclosure: two 7200 RPM HDDs can draw more than the NAS board itself.

**Expandability.** RAM upgrades, NVMe slots, 10GbE expansion, and USB ports for external backup targets all extend the useful life of a NAS.

## Individual Reviews

### 1. Synology DS225+: Best Overall

2-Bay · Intel Celeron J4125 · 2GB DDR4 (expandable to 6GB) · €320-350

The DS225+ is the NAS I'd recommend to most people who ask. It is the direct successor to the DS224+, with the same J4125 CPU and 2GB base RAM and one real upgrade: a 2.5GbE port next to the 1GbE port, replacing the older model's dual 1GbE. It is not the cheapest, the fastest, or the most feature-rich. It is the one that works without drama for years.

DSM is the reason to buy this NAS. The interface is polished, the backup applications are mature, and the package catalog (Container Manager, Surveillance Station, media serving) covers the common use cases without feeling bloated. Hyper Backup handles encrypted, deduplicated backups to local drives, a remote Synology NAS, and cloud targets (S3, Backblaze B2, Google Drive) from one application.

The J4125 is not fast. It is a 4-core embedded chip designed for low power. For file serving, backup, and Docker containers running lightweight services, it is perfectly adequate. Plex transcoding of 4K HEVC content is where it runs out of headroom. The 2GB base RAM is tight if you run several packages; if you use Docker or run more than two or three services, plan on upgrading to 6GB through the SO-DIMM slot. There are no M.2 slots, so NVMe cache is not an option on this model.

Power draw is low. Synology's spec sheet lists 16.98W in access mode and 6.08W with the drives in hibernation.

**Pros:**
- DSM, the best NAS operating system available
- Excellent power efficiency
- Mature Hyper Backup and Active Backup
- SHR allows mixed drive sizes
- Large community and good documentation

**Cons:**
- Proprietary drive push (HAT5300 drives are expensive)
- Base RAM is only 2GB; upgrade needed for Docker
- J4125 is aging and weak at 4K HEVC transcoding
- No M.2 slots
- Price premium over competitors with similar raw specs

### 2. QNAP TS-264: Best Features

2-Bay · Intel Celeron N5095 · 8GB DDR4 (expandable to 16GB) · €480-510

The TS-264 is the feature-heavy option. Where Synology focuses on reliability and polish, QNAP packs in everything: HDMI 2.1 output, dual 2.5GbE ports, two M.2 NVMe slots, USB 3.2 Gen 2, and a PCIe Gen 3 x2 slot. It is a NAS that tries to be a mini server.

The N5095 is faster than Synology's J4125, and the 8GB base RAM lets you run Docker containers, VMs through Virtualization Station, and QNAP's own apps without an immediate upgrade. The dual 2.5GbE ports support link aggregation, which helps if your switch supports it.

QTS 5 is powerful but less intuitive than DSM. The interface is busier, settings are scattered across more menus, and the default security posture is weaker. QNAP has had several high-profile vulnerabilities in recent years, and the Deadbolt ransomware campaign hit its users hard. Harden a QNAP NAS on day one: disable the default admin account, enable 2FA, and configure the firewall.

The PCIe slot is the other differentiator. It is the only unit in this list that takes a 10GbE card.

**Pros:**
- Most features per euro (HDMI, dual 2.5GbE, dual M.2)
- 8GB base RAM, no immediate upgrade needed
- Virtualization Station for lightweight VMs
- HDMI 2.1 for direct media playback
- PCIe slot for 10GbE expansion

**Cons:**
- QTS is less polished than DSM
- Security track record (Deadbolt, Qlocker)
- Higher idle power than Synology (~15-18W without drives)
- Louder fan than Synology at comparable load
- Thinner support documentation

Where it wins is raw value. 4GB base RAM is double Synology's 2GB, so you can run a few containers before you need an upgrade, and its single 2.5GbE port matches the faster of the DS225+'s two NICs. At roughly €300, two of them cost less than one DS225+, which is a real argument if you want redundancy.

The DS425+ is the NAS you buy when you know your storage needs will grow. Four bays with SHR mean you can start with two drives, add a third later, then a fourth, without rebuilding the array from scratch each time.

## Buyer's Guide

**Buy the Synology DS225+ if:** you want a NAS that runs for 5+ years with minimal maintenance. DSM is the best NAS OS, Hyper Backup handles every common backup scenario, and the ecosystem is proven. This is the default recommendation for most home users.

**Buy the QNAP TS-264 if:** you want the most features in a 2-bay form factor: HDMI output for direct media playback, dual 2.5GbE, 8GB RAM for Docker-heavy use, and a PCIe slot for 10GbE later. Be prepared to harden security on day one.

**Buy the TerraMaster F2-425 if:** budget is the main constraint and you can accept less polished software. The quad-core chip, 16GB RAM ceiling, and 2.5GbE make the hardware good, and TOS is functional if not inspiring. There is no M.2 slot, so skip it if you want NVMe cache.

**For the homelab audience:** a Synology DS225+ with two 4TB drives in SHR-1 (mirroring) is the core of a 3-2-1 backup strategy. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which NAS is best for a homelab?

It depends on what the NAS does in the lab. As the backup target behind your servers, the Synology DS225+ is still the pick, because the backup software matters more than the CPU. If the NAS also runs Docker containers or small VMs, the QNAP TS-264 and the UGREEN DXP2800 are the better homelab NAS devices: both ship with 8GB RAM (up to 16GB) and two M.2 slots, against the DS225+'s 2GB with a 6GB ceiling. If you expect to add disks, the 4-bay Synology DS425+ is the one that grows.

### What is the best QNAP NAS for a homelab?

Of QNAP's range, this guide compares only the TS-264, and it is the most homelab-friendly unit in the list: dual 2.5GbE, 8GB RAM, Virtualization Station for small VMs and the only PCIe slot here for a 10GbE card. The catch is QTS security. Disable the default admin account, turn on 2FA and configure the firewall before the box goes on your network.

### How much storage do I need for home backup?

For most households, 2-4TB covers documents, photos, phone backups, and critical system images. If you archive video or run a media server, plan on 8-16TB. Start with two drives in a mirror (RAID 1 or SHR-1) and expand later; a NAS lets you replace drives one at a time with larger ones. Run your drive sizes through the [RAID capacity calculator](https://woitzik.dev/tools/raid-calculator/) to see what a given array yields once redundancy is accounted for.

### What drives should I put in a NAS?

NAS-rated drives (Seagate IronWolf, WD Red Plus) are designed for 24/7 operation and rated for the vibration of multi-bay enclosures. Desktop drives (WD Blue, Seagate Barracuda) are cheaper but not rated for continuous use. They work, but you give up the workload rating and the warranty terms that matter in a box that never sleeps. For set-and-forget reliability, buy NAS-rated drives.

### Is RAID the same as backup?

No. RAID protects against drive failure, not against deletion, corruption, ransomware, or fire. A NAS in RAID 1 with no offsite copy is one `rm -rf /` away from losing everything. RAID is availability; backup is recoverability. You need both.

### Should I use Synology Hybrid RAID or standard RAID?

Use SHR if you are on Synology. It lets you mix drive sizes without wasting capacity. With two drives, SHR-1 behaves like RAID 1. With four drives, SHR-1 gives you single-drive fault tolerance and lets you swap in larger drives later without rebuilding the entire array.

### Can I run Docker on these NAS devices?

Yes, all five support Docker. Synology's Container Manager (formerly the Docker package) is the most polished implementation. QNAP's Container Station works well but has a more complex interface. TerraMaster's and UGREEN's Docker support is functional but less integrated. If Docker is a primary use case, the QNAP TS-264 (8GB RAM, N5095) or UGREEN DXP2800 (8GB RAM, N100) gives you the best hardware for it.

### How long does a NAS last?

A quality NAS (Synology, QNAP) typically lasts 5-8 years before the hardware becomes obsolete or software support ends. Budget for replacing the drives inside every 3-4 years as a precaution, because drives do not reliably warn you before they fail. The enclosure itself will usually outlast two or three sets of drives.

### Is a NAS worth it versus just using an external hard drive?

For a single computer with non-critical data, an external drive is fine. For a multi-device household, data you cannot afford to lose, or a homelab with services that need shared storage, a NAS is worth it. The difference is automation: a NAS handles RAID, snapshots, cloud sync, and multi-user access on its own. An external drive only works as a backup if you remember to plug it in, and that strategy fails the first time you forget.

## Conclusion

The NAS market in 2026 comes down to one question: do you trust the software or the hardware more? Synology's DS225+ has aging hardware and the most dependable software. The UGREEN DXP2800 has excellent hardware and unproven software. The QNAP TS-264 sits in between, with strong hardware and software that needs active security management.

For a home backup NAS that runs 24/7 for the next five years, trust matters more than specs. The DS225+ is the boring choice, and boring is what you want from the device holding your only copy of family photos and important documents.

Buy the DS225+. Put two IronWolf drives in it. Configure Hyper Backup to sync to a cloud target. Verify the restore works once. Then leave it alone, which is how a backup system should behave.
